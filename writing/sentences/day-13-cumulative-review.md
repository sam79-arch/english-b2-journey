## Section E: Choose & Explain
1.  **Correct: (B) I have known my manager for five years.**
    *Explanation:* "Know" is a stative verb expressing a state, not an action. Stative verbs are not used in continuous tenses.
2.  **Correct: (A) A number of employees are unhappy with the new policy.**
    *Explanation:* "A number of" is an idiom meaning "several" or "many" and takes a plural verb.
3.  **Correct: (A) She has written three emails this morning.**
    *Explanation:* The Present Perfect Simple is used to emphasize the result or completion of an action and "how many" have been done. The continuous form would emphasize the duration of the activity.
4.  **Correct: (A) or (B). Both can be correct.**
    *Explanation:* Collective nouns like "committee" can take a singular verb (if seen as a single unit) or a plural verb (if seen as a group of individuals). "Have" suggests disagreement or individual actions within the group.
5.  **Correct: (A) He's tired because he has been playing football.**
    *Explanation:* The Present Perfect Continuous is used to emphasize the continuous activity that caused the present result (being tired).
6.  **Correct: (A) The number of students in the class is limited to 20.**
    *Explanation:* "The number of" refers to a single figure (the total) and therefore takes a singular verb.
7.  **Correct: (B) I have lost my keys. I can't find them.**
    *Explanation:* The focus is on the present result of a past action: the keys are currently lost. The continuous form would imply the process of losing them is ongoing, which is illogical.
8.  **Correct: (A) Each of the applicants has to submit a portfolio.**
    *Explanation:* "Each" is a singular pronoun and requires a singular verb, even when followed by a plural noun ("applicants").
9.  **Correct: (B) By the time she was 25, she had started her own company.**
    *Explanation:* The Past Perfect Simple is used for a completed action that happened before another point in the past. The continuous form would imply the action of starting was ongoing, which is less likely.
10. **Correct: (B) I have understood this problem since you explained it.**
    *Explanation:* "Understand" is a stative verb. It describes a mental state and cannot be used in the continuous form.

### **Common Mistakes Reference Box**

| Concept                               | Rule & Explanation                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Example                                                                          |
| :-------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Top 5 S-V Agreement Traps**           | **1. "One of the..."**: Always takes a singular verb (`one is`).<br>**2. "The number of..."**: Refers to a single quantity, takes a singular verb (`the number is`).<br>**3. "A number of..."**: Means "many," takes a plural verb (`a number are`).<br>**4. "Neither/nor, Either/or"**: Verb agrees with the closest noun.<br>**5. Collective Nouns**: (team, committee, family) can be singular (as a unit) or plural (as individuals). | 1. One of my **friends is** here.<br>2. The **number of cases is** rising.<br>3. A **number of cases are** unsolved.<br>4. Neither the manager nor the **employees are** happy.<br>5. The **team is** winning. / The **team are** arguing. |
| **Stative Verbs in Perfect Tenses**     | Stative verbs describe states, not actions (know, believe, understand, own, have, seem, love, hate). They are **not** used in continuous tenses. Use the simple form instead.                                                       | **Incorrect**: I have been knowing her for years.<br>**Correct**: I have known her for years. |
| **"For" vs. "Since" vs. "By the time"** | **For**: Used with a period of time (e.g., `for three years`).<br>**Since**: Used with a specific point in time (e.g., `since 2020`, `since Monday`).<br>**By the time**: Indicates an action was completed before another point in the past (used with Past Perfect). | He has worked here **for** two years.<br>He has worked here **since** 2022.<br>**By the time** I arrived, he **had left**. |
